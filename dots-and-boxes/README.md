# Dots & Boxes

Dots and boxes on a wooden pegboard on a 3D desk, with the flat 2D board as a choice and as the fallback. No build step: open `index.html` in any browser; no network needed. The 3D board loads the shared `../lib/three.min.js` and `../lib/tabletop.js` (see `lib/README.md`), so the folder needs `lib/` beside it. Without it, or without WebGL, the game plays in 2D.

## Rules
Click the gap between two adjacent dots to draw a line. The fourth side of a box claims it for you and you go again; one line can claim two boxes. Otherwise the turn passes to the next seat in order (1 → 2 → … → last → 1). When every line is drawn, the most boxes wins. If the top score is shared the game is a tie; the end screen ranks every player, with equal scores sharing a rank.

## Options
- **Grid** 3×3 to 8×8 dots (4 to 49 boxes). Default 6×6. An odd box count cannot end level, and 6×6 gives 25: measured over 600 games it tied none, where the old 5×5 default (16 boxes, even) tied 2.7%. With four or five players a 6×6 or larger board also gives everyone more to play for.
- **Players** 2 to 5 (default 2). Each seat has its own colour for its lines and boxes: cyan, pink, lime, amber, violet.
- **Seats** seat 1 is always you. Every other seat is either *Human* (hot-seat on the same screen) or the computer at one of three levels:
  - *Easy* plays a random line.
  - *Normal* takes a box when it can, otherwise draws a line that does not give a box away, otherwise anything.
  - *Hard* can also **decline** a box. Taking every box you can reach is the beginner mistake, because whoever finishes a chain has to open the next one; Hard weighs taking against the double-cross — leaving the last two boxes of a chain so the opponent is forced to open the next — by playing each option out. When no safe line is left it opens the chain that hands over the fewest boxes. Against the old greedy Hard it wins 83% on 5×5 and 93% on 6×6 (n=600 each, seats swapped halfway).

  Human seats are named *Player n* (or *You* when you are the only human); computer seats are *Computer* (or *Computer n* when there is more than one).

Options are remembered in the browser. N starts a new game.

## The 3D table
A cherry pegboard with a lighter field, a brass inlay, a shallow groove along every line there is to draw and a brass pin at every dot, on the shared tabletop kit's desk (the look, lights, desk and camera are the kit's, as in Ludo, City Tycoon, Go, Troll Chess and Tic-tac-toe). The pegs always span 30 cm, so a bigger grid has more, closer pegs, and the pins and sticks thin out a little, as the 2D dots keep their size while the cells shrink. The rules, the computer, the seats, the scores and the high scores are the 2D page's; the 3D board only draws the game and turns a tap into the same line the 2D canvas would draw.

- **Defaults:** 3D wherever WebGL works. Angled on a desktop or a phone held sideways, top-down on a phone held upright. **Top / Angled** and **2D** buttons sit in the board's top-right corner (the camera leaves a strip of desk there, sized from the stage, so they cover no line); in 2D a **3D** button at the top right of the HUD brings it back. The choice and the camera are remembered in `dgames.dots-and-boxes.view` (localStorage, read and written in try/catch; a corrupt value is ignored). Quality is low on touch screens and high elsewhere; `?q=low` / `?q=high` override it. `?no3d` forces 2D. 2D is also what you get when WebGL is missing, when building the scene throws (the half-built scene is disposed), or when the WebGL context is lost mid-game; in every case the game carries on where it was.
- **Layout:** the 3D canvas covers exactly the 2D canvas's square (which stays in place, invisible), so the portrait, sideways-phone and desktop layouts size it the same way and nothing else on the page moves. Checked against the previous page at 390×844, 375×667, 360×640, 844×390, 667×375 and 1280×800 with 2 to 5 players: the 2D board, the HUD and the stage have the same boxes, every control is at least 44×44 and nothing sits under the back button. No page scroll with two players anywhere, or with three at 390×844 and the sideways and desktop sizes. Three or more players on a 375×667 or 360×640 phone held upright still scroll, as they did before (the board stops at its 240 px floor), and so do four or five at 667×375, where the controls column scrolls and the board stays put.
- **A line** is a lacquered stick in the player's colour that drops from 3.5 cm and bounces into the groove between two pins, rocking a little as it lands; the last line drawn glows warm underneath. **A box** gets a tile in the owner's colour with a pale stamp carrying their seat number (so ownership does not rest on colour alone), which pops up out of the board and lands.
- **Chains and the double-cross:** while a player keeps the turn by taking boxes, the tiles of that run hover a few millimetres up over a glow in their colour, so a chain being taken reads as one growing group; when the turn passes they set down together. One line that closes two boxes at once (the double-cross) pops both tiles together, higher, under a gold flash across the pair. The game's last line lifts the winner's tiles (every leader's, on a tie) in a wave across the board; the result card is the 2D one. A new game sweeps the sticks and tiles off, far ones last; changing the grid rebuilds the board at once. None of it holds up play: the line is in the game the moment it is drawn and the computer answers on the same 480 ms timer as in 2D. Reduced motion skips every animation.
- **Tapping:** the kit's gestures (a drag tilts and turns the camera, a pinch zooms, a still press is a tap; the click a touch tap sends afterwards is dropped, and two quick taps on the board are two taps, not a camera reset). A tap is matched on the screen, not on the board: every peg is projected to the canvas and the nearest line by distance to its segment wins, with the 2D page's reach (22 px or 0.36 of the line, whichever is more; 24 px or 0.42 for a finger), so the far rows of the angled view are not stingier than the near ones. Three refusals keep a tap from drawing a line nobody meant, which the 2D page does not have: a tap nearest a line already drawn draws nothing; a tap about as near two lines (a box's middle; inside a corner unless within ~37° of one of its lines) draws neither; and a tap on a peg draws none of the lines that meet there. A mouse shows the stick under the pointer as a ghost. On a touch screen, when the lines on screen are under 32 px (median, as Go measures its points), the first tap shows the ghost stick and a second tap on it draws it, so a mis-tap costs nothing; that happens on 7×7 and 8×8 on a small phone, in the angled view on a phone held sideways, and when zoomed out. Checked in Chromium: every line of 3×3 and 8×8 maps back to itself, is not under a button, and a real tap on it draws exactly that line, at 360×640, 375×667 and 390×844 top-down, 844×390 and 667×375 angled, 844×390 top-down and 1280×800 angled (12/12 and 112/112 each). Taps on every peg and 3 px and 0.15 of a cell around it drew nothing on 8×8 (0 of 576 at each size); on 3×3 angled, a tap 0.15 of a cell diagonally outside a corner peg picks the outer line beside it (2 of 81), the only line there.
- **Cost:** rendering is on demand, so a still board draws nothing, and the 2D page's frame loop stops while the 3D board is up. A full 8×8 board (112 sticks, 49 tiles) is 15 draw calls including the shadow pass (sticks and each player's tiles are instanced), 52k triangles at low quality and 62k at high. At 4× CPU throttle in headless Chromium on SwiftShader (a software GPU), the whole frame, waiting for SwiftShader to rasterise it, takes 113–177 ms at low and 139–198 ms at high at 360×640 and 844×390; Tic-tac-toe with a full board, measured the same way in the same run, took 60–88 and 74–98 ms. Only animation frames pay it, and a phone GPU draws this scene far faster.

## High scores
When you are the only human at the table and win outright, your margin over the best computer (your boxes minus theirs) is a score. If it places in the top ten you are asked for three-letter initials (**Enter** saves, **Esc** skips). There is one table per computer level, keyed by the hardest computer in that game, and each entry notes the grid, the player count and the box count. **High scores** in the controls shows the table for the current seat setup; it lives below the board so it never resizes the canvas. Games with two or more humans, ties and losses are not recorded. Tables are stored in this browser only (localStorage).

## Tests
`node test/ai.js` loads the game logic out of `index.html` without a browser and:
- checks box completion, double-box completion, extra turns, turn passing and game end;
- checks Normal takes a box when it can and never opens a box while a safe line exists;
- checks Hard, when forced to give away, opens the cheapest chain;
- plays 400-game tournaments between the levels and prints the win / draw / loss mix.

`node test/players.js` plays full games with 2, 3, 4 and 5 players and checks that turns cycle through every seat in order, a completed box keeps the turn, the final scores add up to the number of boxes, and the ranking / winner (or tie) is reported correctly, including scripted three-way ties and an outright win by a late seat.

The 3D board is checked in a real browser (Playwright, not part of `npm test`): tap coverage as above, whole games against the computer by real taps (2 players at 1280×800 and 360×640, 4 players at 844×390 and 360×640, 7×7 at 667×375), fallbacks, switching between 2D and 3D mid-game, and the layout against the previous page.

`node test/hiscore.js` loads the pasted high-score module with a stubbed `localStorage` and checks rank order, the top-ten cap and initials handling; then checks `hiScoreOf()` on scripted games (a lone human winning, losing, two humans, a tie, keyed by the hardest computer) and on 300 random games against the winner and scores.
